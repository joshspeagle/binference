The population, this sample, and each object

What pastry returns beyond ρ, how each piece is computed in results.py (_IndividualMixin, l. 112–315), and what is exact. Step 2.1 §8 has the explorer for population vs realised-sample intervals; this page adds one for individual objects' values.

§2

This sample: realized_counts

Given ρ, the labels are independent: $z_i\mid\rho, D \sim \mathrm{Cat}(r_i(\rho))$. Drawing ρ from its posterior and then all labels gives exact joint draws, and $n_k = \sum_i w_i\,\mathbf 1[z_i = k]$ is the realised histogram. Integer weights (compressed rows) draw $\mathrm{Mult}(w_i, r_i)$ per row; non-integer weights draw one label per row and weight it.

  • Why it's narrower than ρ: ρ carries the multinomial shot noise of having drawn N objects; $n$ doesn't. With precise data, $n$ is nearly known while ρ still has width $\sqrt{\rho_k(1-\rho_k)/N}$ (2.1 §8).
  • With selection it counts the detected objects only. Gibbs already imputes the missed ones, so a "full sample" version (detected + missed) is available once $S$ is absolute (#46).
§3

Each object's bin: assignment_probabilities

$P(z_i = k\mid D) = \mathbb E_{\rho\mid D}[r_{ik}(\rho)]$: the average of the conditional posteriors over (up to 500, evenly spaced) population draws. This is the Rao–Blackwellised estimate, smoother than counting label draws.

  • Shrinkage: the population acts as each object's prior, so a broad likelihood is pulled towards where the population is.
  • Not leave-one-out: the population was fitted including object i. That is the correct full posterior; for checking calibration of individual objects, a leave-one-out version would be the honest one (negligible difference at large N).
  • From a point estimate: a PointEstimate has one "draw", so it returns the plug-in $r_i(\hat\rho)$, which ignores the uncertainty in ρ. Under #35 (draws and a best fit together) both will exist, and they should be labelled differently.
  • Selection: uses $\tilde L$, so it is the posterior of a detected object: right for the catalogue in hand.
§4

Each object's value: sample_values

After drawing a bin, a value inside it follows the object's own likelihood times the population inside the bin, which the model takes to be flat. Three paths in the code:

  • Gaussian inputs: exact truncated normals per axis (stored means and σ).
  • With loglike(z, i): inverse-CDF on 64 sub-points per bin (1-D only; a Python loop over objects).
  • Otherwise: uniform within the bin (top-hat), accurate only if the likelihood is nearly flat across the bin.

Even the "exact" path inherits the flat-within-bin population: the value draws follow a staircase version of the true population. The explorer shows one object's true-value posterior under a known population.

true posteriorhistogram bins (today)linear B-spline basis (#5)population

The population is taken as known exactly (infinite N) to isolate the representation. Histogram: bin masses of the true population, flat inside bins. B-spline: hat functions on the bin edges with weights from the population density at the edges (an interpolation, close to but not exactly the fitted weights). Distances are total variation to the true posterior.

At the defaults (10 bins, σ = half a bin, an object measured just above a narrow population peak) the histogram version is 0.28 away from the true posterior in total variation and its mean is shifted by 0.018, a third of σ; the B-spline version is 0.08 away. With 40 bins both are close (0.06 and 0.015). Broad errors don't fix it: at σ = 2 bins the posterior takes on the population's own shape, so the histogram's staircase stays (0.24) even though its mean is nearly right. The error matters for objects measured near sharp population features, which is where shrinkage matters most.

§5

Findings and proposals

  1. Use each input's own within-bin information. After the input redesign (#31, #32) most inputs carry it: density inputs can evaluate their density inside the bin; sample inputs can resample the object's own samples that fall in the drawn bin, weighted by $1/\pi(z_s)$ (exact under the model, no extra storage beyond the samples); only tables and binned measurements have none, and they should warn when values are requested.
  2. B-spline bases (#5) change the within-bin population from flat to linear, so value draws become $\propto \phi_k(z)\,p(d_i\mid z)$, and the staircase disappears (explorer).
  3. Correlated Gaussians (#25) need joint truncated draws inside the cell, not per-axis ones.
  4. Plug-in vs full (#35): results that hold a best fit and draws expose both per-object answers, clearly named.
  5. Selection (#46, #49): offer realised counts for the full sample (detected + imputed missed) when S is absolute.
  6. Speed: the loglike path loops over objects in Python; vectorise it when it becomes the density path.
