The model in the middle

What PopulationModel computes, why, and where each line lives. Every engine in pastry (NUTS, Gibbs, MAP, Laplace, VB) is a different way of exploring the one posterior defined here.

§1

Generative model and the binning assumption

likelihoods.py · from_gaussian (l. 600), from_function, from_samples

Bins $B_1,\dots,B_K$ with volumes $v_k$ partition the support. The population parameter is one probability per bin:

$$\begin{aligned}\rho&\sim p(\rho), \qquad \rho_k=P(z\in B_k),\\ z_i\mid\rho&\sim\mathrm{Categorical}(\rho),\\ d_i\mid z_i&\sim p(d\mid z).\end{aligned}$$

The data enter through $p(d_i\mid z_i\in B_k)$, an average of $p(d_i\mid z)$ over $z$ inside the bin. That average needs the population's shape within the bin, which $\rho$ does not describe. pastry assumes it is flat in the bin coordinate:

$$L_{ik}\;=\;\frac{1}{v_k}\int_{B_k}p(d_i\mid z)\,dz$$

instead of the exact value under the true population density $f$:

$$p(d_i\mid z_i\in B_k)=\frac{\int_{B_k}p(d_i\mid z)\,f(z)\,dz}{\int_{B_k}f(z)\,dz}.$$
The single approximation. Everything downstream is exact given $L$. When the true density $f$ varies within bins, $L$ is wrong by an amount that depends on $f$ and does not shrink with $N$: with enough objects this binning bias exceeds the statistical error.

§2

Marginal posterior

_kernels.py · loglike_and_counts (l. 47) — likelihoods.py · _normalise_linear (l. 947) — model.py · log_prob_and_grad (l. 171)

Summing over each object's bin:

$$p(d_i\mid\rho)=\sum_k L_{ik}\rho_k=L_i\cdot\rho$$
$$p(\rho\mid D)\;\propto\;p(\rho)\prod_i\,(L_i\cdot\rho)^{w_i}$$

Three properties the design leans on

  1. Row scale is irrelevant. $L_i\to c_iL_i$ adds only $w_i\log c_i$. Rows are stored with maximum 1 and $\log c_i$ kept in log_scale; the model folds $\sum_i w_i\log c_i$ into _ll_const. Mixed units across objects are harmless, and normalisation prevents underflow: $s_i=L_i\cdot\rho\ge\rho_k$ for the bin where row $i$ peaks.
  2. Weights are exponents. Integer $w_i$ are multiplicities (compress(), unfolding counts): exact. Non-integer weights give a weighted pseudo-likelihood whose posterior width means something only if the weights are calibrated.
  3. Log-concave in ρ. A sum of logs of linear functions; with Dirichlet α ≥ 1 the posterior has one mode on the simplex (used by mix-SQP).

§3

Selection

model.py · __init__ (l. 75), _ll_and_deta (l. 152) — selection.py

$S_k$ is the probability that an object in bin $k$ enters the catalogue. Two physically different situations:

"observed" mode

selection acts on
the measured data (e.g. a magnitude cut); $L$ already includes it
likelihood row
$L_{ik}=p(d_i,\mathrm{obs}\mid k)$
per-object likelihood
$\dfrac{L_i\cdot\rho}{S\cdot\rho}$

"true" mode

selection acts on
the true bin only, independent of $d$
likelihood row
$L_{ik}=p(d_i\mid k)$
per-object likelihood
$\dfrac{\sum_k L_{ik}S_k\rho_k}{S\cdot\rho}$

Both give one formula with $\tilde L=L$ or $L\,\mathrm{diag}(S)$:

$$\log p(\rho\mid D)=\log p(\rho)+\sum_i w_i\log(\tilde L_i\cdot\rho)\;-\;W\log(S\cdot\rho).$$

The columns of $L$ are scaled by $S$ once, at model construction (self._L); the normaliser is added in _ll_and_deta. Only ratios of $S$ matter for fractions, so _detect = S / max(S) is used where an absolute probability is needed (imputing missed objects). An array selection requires selection_mode: on a three-bin test the wrong mode gave $(0.23, 0.31, 0.46)$ for a truth of $(0.40, 0.35, 0.25)$.

Change the seed: that error changes direction from mock to mock, while the errors from the wrong treatments stay put. In observed mode there is also a small systematic part, because $S_k$ is itself a bin average computed under the flat-within-bin assumption of §1.

§4

Rates

A Poisson process with expected numbers $\lambda_k$ and exposure $S_k$: the observed count is Poisson$(S\cdot\lambda)$ and

$$p(\lambda\mid D)\propto p(\lambda)\prod_i(\tilde L_i\cdot\lambda)^{w_i}\,e^{-S\cdot\lambda}.$$

With $\lambda=\Lambda\rho$ this factorises into the fraction likelihood times a Poisson likelihood for the total $\Lambda$; fractions are the "conditional on $N$" version. In the explorer above, the last line of the readout is this rates view: with a flat prior, $\hat\Lambda=N_{\rm obs}/(S\cdot\hat\rho)$, the number of objects in the population before selection, with Poisson error $\sqrt{N_{\rm obs}}/(S\cdot\hat\rho)$. Ignoring selection gives $\hat\Lambda=N_{\rm obs}$. Selected by the prior (GammaRates), handled in the kind == "rates" branch of _ll_and_deta.

§5

Gradient: expected counts

$$r_{ik}=\frac{L_{ik}\rho_k}{L_i\cdot\rho},\qquad \hat n_k=\sum_i w_i r_{ik}$$
$$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\eta_k}=\begin{cases}\hat n_k-W\rho_k & \text{fractions}\\[2pt] \hat n_k-W\rho_k\,\dfrac{S_k}{S\cdot\rho} & \text{with selection}\\[2pt] \hat n_k-S_k\lambda_k & \text{rates}\end{cases}$$

$r_{ik}$ is object $i$'s posterior over bins given $\rho$ (its "responsibility"); $\hat n_k$ is EM's E-step. The derivation is two lines. With $\rho=\mathrm{softmax}(\eta)$, $\partial\rho_j/\partial\eta_k=\rho_j(\delta_{jk}-\rho_k)$, so

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta_k}\log(L_i\cdot\rho)=\frac{\sum_j L_{ij}\rho_j(\delta_{jk}-\rho_k)}{L_i\cdot\rho}=r_{ik}-\rho_k .$$

Summing with weights gives $\hat n_k-W\rho_k$. The gradient vanishes when the expected counts match $W\rho$: the population is self-consistent with the objects' own posteriors. The selection term $-W\log(S\cdot\rho)$ contributes $-W\rho_kS_k/(S\cdot\rho)$ in the same way.

Cost. One gradient is the two passes of §2: $s=L\rho$ and $L^\top(w/s)$, then an elementwise product with $\rho$. No $N\times K$ temporary is formed; sparse $L$ costs $O(\text{nnz})$. The prior then only adds its own (cheap) term through the chain rule.

§6

EM, stacking and the certificate

samplers/base.py · em_update (l. 40) — model.py · kkt_gap (l. 258), missing_information (l. 282)

Keeping the labels $z_i$: given $z$, the counts are sufficient and a Dirichlet prior is conjugate, $\rho\mid z\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha+n)$ (Gibbs alternates this with $z\mid\rho$). Marginalising $z$ returns §2 exactly. EM's update is

$$\rho_k\;\leftarrow\;\frac{\hat n_k(\rho)+\alpha_k-1}{W+A-K}.$$
  • Stacking is one EM step. Averaging per-object posteriors computed under an interim prior $\pi$ is exactly $\hat n(\pi)/W$: the first EM iterate from $\rho=\pi$.
  • The MLE is the fixed point of iterated stacking. IBU, EMQ/SLD, Richardson–Lucy and Shepp–Vardi are this iteration.
  • Certificate. At the MLE, $g_k=\frac1W\sum_i w_iL_{ik}/s_i\le1$ with equality where $\hat\rho_k>0$; kkt_gap reports $\max_k g_k-1$.
  • Speed is set by the fraction of missing information $f$: EM's linear rate → $f$, Gibbs autocorrelation time ≈ $(1+f)/(1-f)$.

Why "self-consistent" is not enough

Every fixed point of EM satisfies $\hat\rho_k=\hat n_k/W$, but so does any point with some $\rho_k=0$: EM can never revive a component it has driven to zero, and a stack started from a sparse interim prior stays sparse. The KKT condition adds the missing half: for a zero component, $g_k\le1$ must hold too, meaning no empty bin could increase the likelihood if given a little mass. In the explorer, compare the KKT gap of the stack (large) with EM after a few hundred steps.

The KKT condition, derived

Maximise $\ell(\rho)=\sum_i w_i\log(L_i\cdot\rho)$ subject to $\rho_k\ge0$ and $\sum_k\rho_k=1$. With a multiplier $\mu$ for the sum and $\nu_k\ge0$ for each bound, an optimum must satisfy

$$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\rho_k}=\mu-\nu_k,\qquad \nu_k\ge0,\qquad \nu_k\rho_k=0,\qquad \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\rho_k}=\sum_i\frac{w_iL_{ik}}{L_i\cdot\rho}=W g_k .$$

Multiplying by $\rho_k$ and summing gives $\mu=\sum_k\rho_k\,\partial\ell/\partial\rho_k=W$ (always $\rho\cdot g=1$). So:

$$g_k=1\ \text{where }\rho_k>0,\qquad g_k\le1\ \text{where }\rho_k=0 .$$

Meaning of $g_k$. Moving a sliver $\varepsilon$ of mass toward bin $k$, $\rho\to\rho+\varepsilon(e_k-\rho)$, changes the log-likelihood at rate $W(g_k-1)$. So $g_k>1$ says "bin $k$ wants more mass". For an occupied bin it must be exactly 1 (otherwise mass would flow in or out); for an empty bin it may be below 1 (the data really want it empty) but never above.

Why it is a certificate. $\ell$ is concave, so these conditions are sufficient as well as necessary, and the gap bounds the distance to the optimum: $\ell^*-\ell(\rho)\le W\,(\max_kg_k-1)$ (the Frank–Wolfe duality gap; Lindsay 1983 calls $g_k-1$ the gradient function of a mixture likelihood). EM's fixed-point condition is only $\rho_k(g_k-1)=0$, which any empty bin satisfies; the KKT condition adds "and $g_k\le1$ for the empty ones".

It does not stop bins hitting zero. Zeros can be correct. The certificate tells a correct zero from a stuck one. Example (10 bins, 2000 objects): EM started from a prior with bin 7 set to zero converges with $\rho_7=0$ and $g_7=1.65$, flagged; the true optimum has $\rho_7=0.11$ and is 57 nats better (bound: 1296). Both solutions have bin 10 empty with $g_{10}\approx0.2$: a legitimate zero.

Making EM fast

When $f\to1$ EM needs thousands of passes. pastry uses two remedies (Phase 4 covers them): SQUAREM (Varadhan & Roland 2008) extrapolates along the last two EM steps, then takes a stabilising EM step so the likelihood still never decreases; mix-SQP (Kim et al. 2020) replaces EM by second-order steps on the concave objective and converges in a handful of iterations. It is the default for Dirichlet priors with α ≥ 1.

Audit notes for this section. M-2: missing_information is wrong with selection (0.50 where EM's actual rate is 0.85) and ignores the prior; a corrected formula is in the audit. M-3: kkt_gap returns 0 at a point whose likelihood is −∞ (a NaN is swallowed). M-4: for rates the gap scales with the units of the exposure. M-6: EM's stopping rule is not tied to the KKT gap. M-1: zero-weight rows make EM reset under bootstrap weights.

§7

Unconstrained coordinates

_simplex.py — priors/dirichlet.py · _SimplexBound (l. 51), _BoundDirichlet (l. 174)

Simplex priors use isometric log-ratio coordinates with the $K\times(K-1)$ orthonormal Helmert basis $V$ (columns sum to zero): $\rho=\mathrm{softmax}(Vx)$. The Jacobian of $x\mapsto(\rho_1,\dots,\rho_{K-1})$ is $\sqrt K\prod_k\rho_k$, so

$$\log p_x(x)=\log p(\rho)+\sum_k\log\rho_k+\tfrac12\log K$$

which for $\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha)$ becomes

$$\log p_x(x)=\sum_k\alpha_k\log\rho_k+\text{const}.$$

The exponent is $\alpha_k$, not $\alpha_k-1$: in $x$ there is no boundary singularity even for α < 1, but bins without data get very long tails (why NUTS struggles with small α). Gradient by the chain rule: $\nabla_x=V^\top(\partial\ell/\partial\eta+\alpha-A\rho)$.

Why these coordinates. The logits $\eta$ have $K$ components but $\rho$ only $K-1$ degrees of freedom: adding a constant to every $\eta_k$ leaves $\rho$ unchanged. The Helmert basis spans exactly the zero-sum subspace, so $x$ has $K-1$ components and no redundant direction. Its columns are orthonormal, so Euclidean distance in $x$ is the Aitchison distance between compositions, the same for every bin: no bin is singled out, unlike stick-breaking (order-dependent) or additive log-ratios (a reference bin).

On the simplex the density piles up against the bin-3 edge (it becomes unbounded for α < 1); in $x$ it stays smooth, but a ridge stretches off toward the top of the panel (the direction in which ρ₃ → 0): the empty bin's log-fraction has an exponential tail with rate α, so its 98% interval spans about $\ln 99/\alpha$ e-folds. NUTS has to traverse that with a step size tuned to the narrow directions, which is why small concentrations hit the maximum tree depth and why check() then suggests Gibbs. With α ≥ 1 the tail is short and nothing goes wrong.

def log_prior_and_grad(self, x, cache, value, d_eta):
    log_rho = log_softmax(cache)
    lp, g_eta = self._lp_eta(value, log_rho)        # Dirichlet: Σ α log ρ + c,  α − Aρ
    return lp + self._log_jac_const, self.V.T @ (d_eta + g_eta)

The other families

  • Logistic (smoothness) priors: $x=(\xi,\log\theta)$, with $\eta=\mu+B\,(s(\theta)\odot\xi)$ and $\xi\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$. The map to $\eta$ is linear in $\xi$, so no Jacobian is needed for the field; the hyperparameters are sampled on the log scale, which adds $\log\theta$ to the density. The softmax redundancy is handled by dropping the constant basis vector. NUTS may also re-mix $\xi$ between centred and non-centred forms (Phase 3.2).
  • Rates: $x=\log\lambda$, with Jacobian $\prod_k\lambda_k$; a Gamma$(a,b)$ prior becomes $\sum_k(a_k\log\lambda_k-b_k\lambda_k)$, the same "exponent $a$, not $a-1$" shift.
  • Engines that do not use $x$: Gibbs, EM, mix-SQP and VB work directly on $\rho$ (or $\lambda$) with conjugate updates, which is why they handle α < 1 gracefully.
§8

Population versus realised sample

results.py · realized_counts (l. 177), assignment_probabilities (l. 134), sample_assignments, sample_values

Two different questions can be asked of the same data:

the population, ρ

question
What distribution were these objects drawn from?
uncertainty includes
measurement noise and the shot noise of having only $N$ objects: even with perfect measurements, $\rho_k$ is uncertain by $\approx\sqrt{\rho_k(1-\rho_k)/N}$.
use it for
physics of the parent population, predictions for new objects, comparing surveys.

the realised sample, $n_k/N$

question
What is the histogram of the true values of these $N$ objects?
uncertainty includes
measurement noise only: with perfect measurements it is known exactly.
use it for
properties of a specific sample, e.g. the redshift distribution of a lensing source sample.

The hierarchical model gives both from the same posterior. Draws of $\rho$ describe the population; for each draw, labels $z_i\sim\mathrm{Cat}(r_i(\rho))$ give the realised counts $n_k=\sum_iw_i\,1[z_i=k]$ (realized_counts). Together these are exact joint draws of $(\rho,z)$.

With σ = 0.01 the realised histogram is almost exact (narrow amber bars around the dots) while ρ keeps the shot-noise width. Raise σ toward the bin width (0.3) and the two converge: the measurement noise dominates both. Increasing N shrinks the population bars as $1/\sqrt N$.

Individual objects

The same draws give each object's posterior given its own data and the population, $p(z_i=k\mid D)=\mathbb E_\rho[r_{ik}(\rho)]$ (assignment_probabilities). It is narrower than the object's own likelihood wherever the population is informative: shrinkage. sample_values then draws a continuous true value inside the drawn bin from the object's own likelihood (exact for Gaussian errors), again using the flat-within-bin assumption of §1.
