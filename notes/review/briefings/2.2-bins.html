Bins, volumes and binning bias

What pastry assumes about the support, where bin sizes matter, and how large the one approximation of the framework (flat within each bin) becomes as data accumulate. File: bins.py (338 lines), plus the places that read bins.volumes.

§1

What a Bins object is

bins.py · Bins (l. 25), constructors (l. 99–157), index (l. 255), coarsen (l. 319)

A Bins is an immutable description of the support. Three kinds:

1-D bins

build with
Bins(edges), Bins.linear(lo, hi, n), Bins.log(lo, hi, n), Bins.from_centers(c)
knows
edges, centres (midpoints in the coordinate), widths, volumes = widths

N-D grids

build with
Bins.grid(edges_x, edges_y, ...): any rectilinear grid, unequal spacing allowed per axis
knows
per-axis edges, shape, cell volumes = products of widths; cells are numbered in C order (last axis fastest); unflatten / flatten convert

categories

build with
Bins.categories(K or labels)
knows
labels only; volumes are 1, no edges, no neighbours; smoothness priors refuse them
  • Membership rule (index): bins are left-closed, and the last bin on each axis also includes its upper edge. Values outside the support get −1.
  • from_centers puts edges at midpoints and extends the outer bins by half a spacing: the right conversion when PDFs are tabulated on a grid of points.
  • coarsen(factor) merges blocks of adjacent bins and returns a fine→coarse map, which result.rebin uses to propagate uncertainties exactly.
  • Immutability: edges are read-only arrays and attributes cannot be reassigned, so a Bins can be shared safely between likelihoods, priors and results (and compared with ==).

§2

Where volumes enter

With equal bins, volumes are irrelevant: every place they enter multiplies by a constant. With unequal bins (log spacing, a coarse tail, N-D cells of different sizes) each of these matters:

placewhat the volume does
likelihood rowsrepresentation="mass" divides by $v_k$ to get the bin-averaged likelihood $L_{ik}$ (a mass is $v_kL_{ik}$)
interim priorsa density at bin centres becomes a mass by multiplying by $v_k$ before dividing it out of posteriors
outputsresult.density() = $\rho_k/v_k$: what a plot of an $n(z)$ shows
priorsDirichlet.from_base_measure and PolyaTree use volumes as the base measure; logistic priors use $\log v_k$ as the default mean (a flat density). A symmetric Dirichlet(α) ignores them.
mockstrue bin masses are density × volume

The mass/density distinction is the most common user error with unequal bins; pastry warns when rows look like the wrong representation (audit I-1 found one place where an interim-prior array is read inconsistently).

§3

Flat in which coordinate?

Bins.log(0.05, 3, 20) gives log-spaced edges, but the coordinate is still $z$: volumes are widths in $z$, centres are midpoints in $z$, and the bin-averaged likelihood assumes the population is flat in $z$ inside each bin. For a wide log bin that is a strong statement: a population falling like $1/z$ (flat in $\log z$) changes by the bin's full ratio across it.

$$\text{flat in }z:\ \ \varphi_k(z)=\frac{1}{v_k},\qquad \text{flat in }\log z:\ \ \varphi_k(z)=\frac{1}{z\,\ln(z_{k+1}/z_k)}\qquad(z\in B_k).$$

Either is a valid "bin shape"; the explorer below compares them. The general version is a measure attached to the bins (Lebesgue in $z$, in $\log z$, or a user density), from which every component would take its geometry: likelihood averaging, base measures, the GP's coordinate and the random walk's spacing. That is the design question parked after step 2.1.

§4

Binning bias, exactly

With infinitely many objects the maximum-likelihood fit converges to the bin weights that best explain the distribution of the data, $p(d)=\int p(d\mid z)f(z)\,dz$:

$$\rho^\infty=\arg\max_\rho\int p(d)\,\log\Big(\sum_k\rho_k\,L_k(d)\Big)\,dd,\qquad L_k(d)=\int p(d\mid z)\,\varphi_k(z)\,dz .$$

If the bin shapes $\varphi_k$ could reproduce $f$ exactly, $\rho^\infty$ would be the true bin masses. They cannot, so $\rho^\infty$ differs from the truth by a bias that does not depend on $N$. The statistical error shrinks as $\sigma_k/\sqrt N$ (from the Fisher information of the same integral), so the two cross at

$$N_{\rm crit}=\min_k\frac{\sigma_k^2}{(\rho^\infty_k-\rho^{\rm true}_k)^2}.$$

Below $N_{\rm crit}$ binning bias is negligible; above it, it dominates. The explorer computes both exactly by quadrature (no simulation noise).

Things to try: the falling exponential with flat-in-z bins: the bias overtakes the statistical error at about 1,500 objects (σ = 0.15, K = 10). Switch to linear B-splines and that rises to about 11,000; for the two-peak population, from about 850 to 35,000. On log bins, the power law ($1/z$, flat in log z) under "flat in z" gives about 56,000, and under "flat in log z" the bias vanishes (the shape is exact). A smaller σ helps histograms only modestly (exponential: 1,500 → 2,800 for σ = 0.15 → 0.05). "Flat in log z" is only offered for log spacing, since a linear grid starts at z = 0.

§5

Choosing bins

  • Narrower than the population's features, or the bias of §4 grows; and, with a flat Dirichlet, not so many that the prior pseudo-counts (one per bin) matter against $N$.
  • Error scale vs bin width sets two things: the size of the binning bias (larger when σ is comparable to the bin) and the missing information (EM and Gibbs slow down as σ grows relative to bins).
  • Smoothness priors make fine bins cheap in statistical terms: they share strength between neighbours, so more bins add resolution without adding noise.
  • Coverage: objects with likelihood mass outside the bins are truncated to the support (rows are renormalised); drop_empty handles objects entirely outside.

The binning-bias diagnostic agreed in step 2.1 (#6) would automate §4 for a real fit: refit on refined bins or another bin shape and flag shifts larger than the error bars. The explorer's $N_{\rm crit}$ is the closed-form version for Gaussian errors.
