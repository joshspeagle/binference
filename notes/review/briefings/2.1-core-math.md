# Briefing 2.1: The math in the middle, and where it lives in the code

Level L3. Each section states the math, then points at the lines that
implement it. Files: `model.py`, `_kernels.py`, `_simplex.py`,
`priors/dirichlet.py` (`_SimplexBound`, `_BoundDirichlet`), `samplers/base.py`.

## 1. Generative model

Objects i = 1..N have true values z_i (continuous, possibly N-D, or a
category). Bins B_1..B_K partition the support; v_k = |B_k|.

    ρ ~ p(ρ)                       population fractions, ρ_k = P(z ∈ B_k)
    z_i | ρ ~ Categorical(ρ)       (bin of object i)
    d_i | z_i ~ p(d | z)            measurement

**The binning assumption.** Within a bin the population is taken to be flat
in the bin coordinate: p(z | z ∈ B_k) = 1/v_k. Then the only thing the data
enter through is the **bin-averaged likelihood**

    L_ik = p(d_i | z_i ∈ B_k) = (1/v_k) ∫_{B_k} p(d_i | z) dz .

This is the single approximation of the whole framework (everything below is
exact given it). When the true population varies within a bin, the effective
L_ik is wrong in a way that depends on the population, and the error does
*not* shrink with N, so with many objects it eventually dominates the
statistical error ("binning bias", Phase 2.2; tested in `test_slow.py`).

## 2. Marginal likelihood and posterior

Summing over the bin of each object:

    p(d_i | ρ) = Σ_k L_ik ρ_k = L_i·ρ
    p(ρ | D) ∝ p(ρ) Π_i (L_i·ρ)^{w_i}

- **Weights** w_i: integer weights are multiplicities (identical rows merged
  by `compress()`, unfolding counts); real weights give a weighted
  likelihood. W = Σ w_i.
- **Row scale invariance.** Multiplying row i by c_i multiplies the
  likelihood by c_i^{w_i}, a constant. So rows are stored with max 1
  (`Likelihoods._values`) and the log-scales kept apart
  (`Likelihoods.log_scale`, summed into `model._ll_const`). Only absolute
  quantities (evidence, held-out scores) need them. This is also why mixed
  units/normalisations across objects are harmless.
- The likelihood is **log-concave in ρ** (sum of logs of linear functions).
  With a Dirichlet prior with α_k ≥ 1 the posterior is log-concave on the
  simplex; this is what mix-SQP exploits (Phase 4.4).

## 3. Selection (fractions)

S_k = probability that an object in bin k makes it into the catalogue.

- **"observed" mode.** Selection acts on the *data* (e.g. a magnitude cut),
  and L_ik = p(d_i, observed | k) already includes it (or the cut is a
  deterministic function of d_i, so the likelihood at the observed d_i is
  unchanged). S_k = ∫ p(d, obs | z) dd averaged over bin k. Then

      p(d_i | observed, ρ) = (L_i·ρ) / (S·ρ)

- **"true" mode.** Detection depends on the true bin only: P(obs | k) = S_k,
  independently of d. Then p(d_i, obs | ρ) = Σ_k L_ik S_k ρ_k, so

      p(d_i | observed, ρ) = Σ_k L_ik S_k ρ_k / (S·ρ)

Both give log p(ρ|D) = log p(ρ) + Σ w_i log(L̃_i·ρ) − W log(S·ρ), with
L̃ = L (observed) or L·diag(S) (true). Code: `PopulationModel.__init__`
builds `self._L` (columns scaled by S once, for "true" mode); the
normaliser is added in `_ll_and_deta`. Only ratios of S matter for fractions;
`self._detect = S / max(S)` is used where an absolute probability is needed
(imputing missed objects in Gibbs/EM/VB).

Getting the mode wrong biases ρ badly (spec §3.6 has a 3-bin example), which
is why an array `selection` requires an explicit `selection_mode`.

## 4. Rates

With a Poisson process of intensity λ_k per bin (expected numbers), and
exposure/selection S_k, the number observed is Poisson(S·λ) and

    p(λ | D) ∝ p(λ) Π_i (L̃_i·λ)^{w_i} exp(−S·λ)

Writing λ = Λ ρ, this factorises into the fraction likelihood times a
Poisson likelihood for the total Λ. Fractions are the "conditional on N"
version. Code: `_ll_and_deta`, branch `kind == "rates"`; prior
`GammaRates` (Phase 3.4).

## 5. Gradient: expected counts

Define per-object evidences s_i = L_i·ρ and **responsibilities**

    r_ik = L_ik ρ_k / s_i       (posterior of object i's bin given ρ)
    n̂_k = Σ_i w_i r_ik = ρ_k Σ_i w_i L_ik / s_i      (expected counts)

With logits η (ρ = softmax(η)):

    ∂ℓ/∂η_k = n̂_k − W ρ_k                                (no selection)
            = n̂_k − W ρ_k S_k / (S·ρ)                    (selection)
            = n̂_k − S_k λ_k        (rates, η = log λ)

Everything costs two passes over L: s = Lρ and Lᵀ(w/s).
Code: `_kernels.loglike_and_counts` (lines ~47–75): `s = matvec(L, rho)`,
`ll = w·log s`, `n_hat = rho * rmatvec(L, w / s)`; it returns −inf if any
s_i ≤ 1e-250 (an "impossible" object). `PopulationModel._ll_and_deta` adds
the selection/rate terms.

## 6. The augmented (hierarchical) form and EM

Keeping the labels z_i: given z, the counts n_k = Σ w_i 1[z_i=k] are
sufficient, and a Dirichlet prior is conjugate: ρ | z ~ Dir(α + n). This is
what Gibbs uses (alternate z | ρ ~ Cat(r_i), then ρ | z). Marginalising z
gives back §2 exactly, so the two forms are the same posterior.

EM on z gives the update ρ_k ← (n̂_k + α_k − 1) / (W + A − K):

- **Stacking is one EM step.** The familiar "stacked n(z)" (average of
  per-object posteriors computed under an interim prior π) is exactly
  n̂(π)/W, the first EM iterate from ρ = π. It is biased toward π whenever
  likelihoods are broad compared with population features.
- **The MLE is a fixed point of iterated stacking**; IBU, EMQ/SLD,
  Richardson–Lucy and Shepp–Vardi are all this iteration.
- **Optimality certificate (KKT).** At the MLE, g_k = (1/W) Σ w_i L_ik/s_i
  ≤ 1 with equality where ρ̂_k > 0. `model.kkt_gap` reports max_k g_k − 1
  (with prior and selection terms included).
- **Speed is set by the missing information**: when likelihoods are broad,
  most information about ρ is "missing" (in the labels), EM's linear rate
  → 1 and the Gibbs autocorrelation time ~ (1+f)/(1−f).
  `model.missing_information` computes the largest generalised eigenvalue of
  (missing info, complete info).

Code: `samplers/base.em_update` (the selection version imputes the missed
objects' expected counts W ρ_k(1−S_k)/(S·ρ)); `prior.map_from_counts`.

## 7. Unconstrained coordinates (what NUTS/Laplace/L-BFGS see)

Engines work on x ∈ R^dim; the prior maps x → η → ρ = softmax(η) (or
λ = exp(η)). The density in x needs the Jacobian.

- **Simplex priors use isometric log-ratio (ILR) coordinates**:
  η = V x with V the K×(K−1) orthonormal Helmert basis (columns sum to zero),
  ρ = softmax(Vx). The Jacobian of x → (ρ_1..ρ_{K−1}) is √K Π_k ρ_k, so

      log p_x(x) = log p(ρ) + Σ_k log ρ_k + ½ log K .

  For Dir(α): log p_x = Σ_k α_k log ρ_k + const. Note the exponent is α_k,
  not α_k − 1: the density in x has no boundary singularity even for α < 1,
  but the posterior then has very long tails in x for bins without data
  (why NUTS struggles with small α; Phase 4).
- **Chain rule**: gradient wrt x = Vᵀ(∂ℓ/∂η + ∂log p_x/∂η), and for Dirichlet
  ∂log p_x/∂η_k = α_k − A ρ_k (A = Σα). Code: `_SimplexBound.forward`
  (`eta = V @ x`) and `log_prior_and_grad` (`V.T @ (d_eta + g_eta)`),
  `_BoundDirichlet._lp_eta`.
- **Logistic priors** use x = (whitened field coefficients, log-hyperparameters):
  η = μ + B (s(θ) ⊙ ξ); no Jacobian for η itself (it is Gaussian), only for
  log θ. Phase 3.2.
- **Rates**: x = log λ.

`PopulationModel.log_prob_and_grad(x)`:
`forward → value (softmax/exp) → _ll_and_deta → prior.log_prior_and_grad`
(about 12 lines). This one function is all NUTS, Laplace and L-BFGS use.

## 8. What the posterior over ρ does and does not mean

- ρ is the **population** the objects were drawn from: it includes the
  multinomial "shot noise" of having only N objects. The histogram of
  *these* objects' true bins is a different quantity (`realized_counts`,
  Phase 2.5); with precise data it is nearly known while ρ is not.
- Individual objects: r_ik(ρ) averaged over the posterior of ρ is each
  object's posterior given its own data *and* the population (shrinkage).

## 9. Points to discuss

1. The flat-within-bin assumption: should pastry offer a correction (e.g.
   sub-bin quadrature with a piecewise-linear population, or a
   "bias check" by refining bins)?
2. Weighted (non-integer) likelihoods: a pseudo-likelihood, so the posterior
   width is only meaningful if weights are calibrated. Warn? Restrict?
3. Selection modes: are "observed"/"true" the right names, and is the
   formalism general enough (selection depending on both z and d)?
4. Rates vs fractions as separate "kinds" vs one model with an optional total.
5. ILR vs other simplex maps (stick-breaking, softmax with a reference bin):
   ILR is symmetric across bins and orthonormal, which keeps the metric
   isotropic; stick-breaking is order-dependent.
